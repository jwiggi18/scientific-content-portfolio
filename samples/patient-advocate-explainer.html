Exome and Genome Sequencing: A Guide for Patients and Families

What these tests look for, what they reliably find, where they fall short, and what to do when results are uncertain — written for patients, families, and advocates navigating the genetic testing process.

What these tests are

Exome and genome sequencing are genetic tests that read your DNA to look for changes that might explain a health condition.

Your DNA contains instructions your body uses to build proteins and regulate how cells work.

DNA in the cell's nucleus carries instructions. Those instructions are used to build proteins — the molecules that carry out most functions in your body.

Changes in those instructions — called variants — can sometimes cause disease.

Each letter represents one unit of the genetic code (A, T, G, or C). A variant is a change in one or more of these letters — sometimes harmless, sometimes significant.

Most disease-causing variants identified to date sit in protein-coding regions of the genome — these are what exome sequencing looks at. Whole genome sequencing extends coverage to non-coding regions, areas that regulate gene activity, which matters in a subset of cases where the disease-causing change is in those regulatory regions rather than in a coding gene.

What sequencing can detect — and what it misses

These tests reliably find small DNA changes: single-letter typos in the genetic code (see variants above), and small insertions or deletions, often called 'indels'.

What they typically do not detect:

  • Large structural rearrangements and copy number changes — unless the lab has added specific analysis for these
  • Repeat expansions — the type of mutation behind conditions like Fragile X, Huntington's disease, and myotonic dystrophy
  • Mosaic changes — variants present in only some cells, not all, which can occur at low enough levels to be invisible
  • Regions of the genome that are difficult to sequence accurately due to repetitive structure
A negative result means the test didn't find a variant it was designed to find. It doesn't mean your symptoms aren't real or don't have a genetic basis. It means the answer, if there is one, is somewhere the test didn't look — or isn't findable with current technology.

Understanding your results

Clinical labs classify genetic variants into five categories based on how much evidence exists linking them to disease:

!
Pathogenic / Likely pathogenic
Strong evidence that this variant causes disease, consistent with your medical situation. This is the result that can lead to a diagnosis.
?
Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)
The current evidence isn't enough to say whether this causes disease. This is not a diagnosis — it's an open question.
⚠ Should not drive medical decisions on its own
✓
Likely benign / Benign
Not expected to contribute to disease.

The category that confuses people most is VUS. Getting a VUS result is common, particularly in exome and genome sequencing, which cast a wide net and pick up many variants with unknown significance.

A VUS should not drive clinical decisions. That includes decisions about surgery, screening, or whether family members should be tested.

A few other things to know about VUS results:

  • VUS classifications change. A variant that's uncertain today may be reclassified — as pathogenic or as benign — as more data accumulate. Labs sometimes recontact patients when this happens, but not always. If you received a VUS result more than a year or two ago, it's worth asking your provider or genetic counselor to check whether the classification has been updated.
  • Family members don't need to be tested for a VUS just because you have one. That changes if and when the VUS is reclassified as pathogenic.

When results don't explain your symptoms

A negative result — where the lab doesn't identify a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant — is the most common outcome of diagnostic sequencing. It doesn't mean your workup is over.

Some paths forward after an inconclusive result:

  • Re-analysis — as databases grow and more variants are characterized, labs can reanalyze existing sequencing data with updated information. Many results that were initially inconclusive become informative with reanalysis. Your provider can request this.
  • Additional testing — sequencing misses certain variant types. Depending on your clinical picture, your provider might recommend tests that target what sequencing doesn't capture.
  • Specialist referral — if you haven't already seen a clinical geneticist or genetic counselor, this is the right time.

What genetic results mean for family members

Many genetic variants are inherited. A pathogenic or likely pathogenic result in you may be relevant for biological relatives — parents, siblings, children — who could be at increased risk. This is called cascade testing.

A genetic counselor can help you understand which family members might benefit from testing, how to approach those conversations, and what testing options exist for relatives. VUS findings, as noted above, should not trigger cascade testing unless and until they are reclassified.

Questions to ask your provider

Before testing:

  • Why is exome or genome sequencing being recommended for my situation rather than a more targeted test?
  • What kinds of results are most likely, and what would each mean for my care?
  • What won't this test be able to tell me?
  • Are there implications for my family members regardless of the result?

After receiving results:

  • What does this result mean specifically for my diagnosis and care plan?
  • If the result is a VUS: what's the process for following up as the evidence evolves?
  • If the result is negative: what are the next steps? Is reanalysis or additional testing worth pursuing?
  • Should any family members be tested based on this finding?

FAQ

Will these tests always give me a diagnosis?

No. Diagnostic rates vary by condition, but even in the best circumstances, exome and genome sequencing identifies a definitive genetic cause in roughly 25–35% of cases where a genetic condition is suspected. Many results are inconclusive or negative, especially on first analysis. That number improves with reanalysis over time.

Why might my doctor recommend genome sequencing instead of exome sequencing?

Usually because prior testing — including exome sequencing — didn't find an answer, or because the clinical picture suggests a non-coding variant might be involved. Genome sequencing is more expensive and generates more data, so it's typically reserved for situations where the broader coverage is warranted.

How will I know if my VUS result gets reclassified?

You may not be notified automatically — it depends on the lab and your provider's practices. The safest approach is to ask your provider or genetic counselor to check on any VUS result that's more than one to two years old.

Who is the right person to explain my results to me?

A genetic counselor. Genetic counselors are specifically trained to translate sequencing results into plain language, explain what findings mean for your health and your family, and help you decide on next steps. If you don't have one, ask your ordering provider for a referral.
