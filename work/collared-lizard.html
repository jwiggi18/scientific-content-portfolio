My research on this animal

I spent several years studying Crotaphytus collaris — the Eastern Collared Lizard, Oklahoma's state reptile — as part of my doctoral work at Oklahoma State University. My research focused on two intertwined questions: what role does early-life coloration play in sexual selection, and does this species have sex chromosomes?

Male hatchlings develop bright orange lateral bars in the fall — months before they're sexually mature. These bars aren't decoration. They function as social signals: males with more developed bars establish territories earlier, treat female hatchlings less aggressively (initiating social bonds that pay off at the next breeding season), and ultimately sire more offspring. This is precocial sexual selection — sexual selection operating before puberty, with fitness consequences that unfold later. The coloration disappears as males reach sexual maturity and transition to the adult color pattern. My dissertation documented the costs and benefits of this system over multiple breeding seasons, tracking individuals from hatch to reproduction.

The sex chromosome work emerged from the same long-term dataset. C. collaris has temperature-influenced sex determination — incubation temperature can bias offspring sex ratios — which made the discovery of sex chromosome signatures all the more interesting. Using gene dose analysis, we found evidence for sex chromosomes in a species where temperature can still override them. That paper was published in Herpetologica in 2020.

This project: mapping iNaturalist sightings

Oklahoma is core range for C. collaris, and I wanted to see what the public iNaturalist record looks like — where sightings concentrate, how data quality is distributed, and whether the patterns in the data match biology or something else. The answer turns out to be partly both, which makes it a useful case study in what observational datasets can and can't tell you.

The data

Observations pulled from the iNaturalist Observations API, filtered to Crotaphytus collaris in Oklahoma. Records span 1979–2026, though meaningful data density begins in the 2010s as iNaturalist adoption grew. The dataset was fetched programmatically, flattened from JSON to CSV, and cleaned prior to mapping: coordinate missingness removed, captive/cultivated records excluded, date fields parsed. 1,759 georeferenced observations total; 1,735 (98.6%) carry research grade status.

Where the sightings are

The map below shows all observations colored by quality grade. Zoom and hover for details — research grade points (blue) have photo-confirmed identifications; casual (red) were submitted without sufficient evidence for community review.

Two clusters dominate: the southwest corner around the Wichita Mountains Wildlife Refuge, and the northeast around the Osage Reservation. Both are well-known wildlife destinations with active naturalist communities. The interior of the state — large stretches of grassland and scrub that likely support the species — shows almost no observations.

What I expected to find — and what the data said

I wrote the expected pattern before running the analysis.

What I expected: sightings concentrated in areas with exposed rock scattered throughout the state and at known habitat (Wichita Mountains, Arcadia Dam); sparse coverage in the panhandle and north-central areas.

What I found: Wichita Mountains cluster confirmed — but the Osage Reservation cluster in the northeast was not what I predicted. The Osage sits near the northeastern edge of the species' range, but sighting density there rivals the Wichita Mountains. That's likely a sampling artifact: the Osage is a major destination for wildlife photographers and birders, and observer effort — not lizard density — might be driving the signal.

The near-total dominance of research-grade observations (98.6%) is worth noting too. The research-grade filter improves data quality, but it also systematically favors locations with active observer communities. The filtering that makes the data more reliable also makes it less representative of the full range.

Observations over time

The temporal pattern shows near-zero submissions before 2010, then a steep rise as iNaturalist gained users — with pronounced activity through the early 2020s. The growth reflects platform adoption and likely not change in lizard populations.

Any trend analysis treating the full 1979–2026 dataset as continuous would be misleading. The pre-2010 records are real but entered retroactively and not comparable in density or spatial coverage to the modern data.

Where the data stops being useful

This dataset maps where sightings occur — it cannot tell us where the species is absent. Sighting absence in a grid cell is not evidence of species absence; it may mean no observer visited with a camera and an iNaturalist account. Given what I know about the biology and field sites for this species, there are almost certainly significant populations in areas with near-zero iNaturalist coverage.

What I'd dig into next

GBIF comparison. iNaturalist feeds into GBIF, but GBIF also aggregates museum records and systematic surveys. Comparing the two would surface where observational coverage is likely insufficient and where older records fill gaps.

Observer effort normalization. Dividing sighting counts by total iNaturalist observations of any species in the same area and period would produce a corrected occurrence rate — reducing the confounding effect of popular wildlife areas.

Habitat overlay. Overlaying land cover or ecoregion data would let you ask whether clustering matches habitat type or human access patterns, and where suitable-but-unsampled habitat exists.

The code

Data fetched from the iNaturalist API, cleaned and mapped in Python. The fetch script and processed CSV are in the repository.

import requests, json, time
import pandas as pd

BASE_URL = "https://api.inaturalist.org/v1/observations"

def fetch_observations(taxon_id, place_id, per_page=200):
    all_results, page = [], 1
    while True:
        params = {"taxon_id": taxon_id, "place_id": place_id,
                  "per_page": per_page, "page": page,
                  "order": "desc", "order_by": "created_at"}
        r = requests.get(BASE_URL, params=params)
        data = r.json()
        results = data.get("results", [])
        if not results:
            break
        all_results.extend(results)
        if len(all_results) >= data["total_results"]:
            break
        page += 1
        time.sleep(1)
    return all_results

# Crotaphytus collaris taxon_id = 36226; Oklahoma place_id = 59
records = fetch_observations(taxon_id=36226, place_id=59)

rows = []
for obs in records:
    coords = obs.get("location", "").split(",")
    rows.append({
        "id": obs.get("id"),
        "observed_on": obs.get("observed_on"),
        "quality_grade": obs.get("quality_grade"),
        "latitude": float(coords[0]) if len(coords) == 2 else None,
        "longitude": float(coords[1]) if len(coords) == 2 else None,
        "common_name": obs.get("taxon", {}).get("preferred_common_name"),
        "captive": obs.get("captive"),
    })

df = pd.DataFrame(rows)
df.to_csv("inat_collared_lizard_oklahoma.csv", index=False)

Data: iNaturalist Observations API, public endpoint, no registration required. Full fetch script and processed CSV available in the data-portfolio/projects/lizard/ directory of this repository.
